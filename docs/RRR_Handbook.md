> **You do not collect the fastest roach. You build the roach that can win this track.**

The foundation is a **kit-racing game**: inspect a circuit, assemble a competitor, test it, diagnose its failures, change individual parts, and race again. Fighting adds another tuning problem; it must not replace racing with an insect arena battle.

For the exact mapping, this handbook uses **Like a Dragon Gaiden’s Pocket Circuit** customization system, then separately incorporates the physical kit-building depth of **Tamiya Mini 4WD**. SEGA’s official customization image supplies a complete reference build; Tamiya supplies the underlying drivetrain, handling, and modular-course references. These are distinct reference layers—not a claim that every Pocket Circuit installment has identical systems. ([『龍が如く』公式ポータルサイト｜SEGA][1])

All RRR names, graft mechanisms, classes, and performance relationships below are proposed game design. Real insect anatomy supplies inspiration, not evidence that these grafts would work. Numerical examples are initial specifications, not tested balance.

# PART I — BUILD THE RACER

## 1. A complete Pocket Circuit build, translated 1:1

The following car appears in SEGA’s official Gaiden customization screenshot. Its displayed build cost is **40**. This is a documented example, **not a claimed winning setup**. The RRR translation preserves every slot and its example cost so the mapping can be checked directly. ([『龍が如く』公式ポータルサイト｜SEGA][1])

Color and decals become **shell stains, race numbers, breeder stamps, and faction markings**, rather than additional performance equipment.

The important lesson is the interaction: the Redline supplies power, the Lowbox trades some theoretical speed for usable thrust, the reservoir supports sustained demand, and the handling components attempt to keep that power on the track.

Removing the handling equipment does not simply make a cheaper version of the same racer. It changes whether the build can finish.

**Graft Points are a sporting budget—not money, mass, or fuel.** Those four quantities remain separate. A part can be cheap to buy, expensive to field, heavy to carry, and economical to run.

## 2. What a rad roach actually is

Thirty years ago, a local rivalry produced three inventions: **the bullhorn ram, the mantis counter-grip, and the grasshopper escape jump**.

Those remain the sport’s founding arguments:

> **Overpower the obstacle. Control the obstacle. Bypass the obstacle.**

Now a **lanista** is the breeder, kit-builder, tuner, and race manager responsible for a stable of named competitors. The roach remains the living foundation. Its donor components are collectively called **sects**; a packaged, installable component is a **sect module**.

**Proposed continuity fix:** roaches were the only insects to survive the entire nuclear winter *on the exposed surface*. Other donor insects were subsequently reintroduced from sealed brood vaults; human survivors emerged from shelters. This retains the roaches’ exceptional survival without eliminating their donors or the wastelanders.

The rad roach’s defining mutation is a **molt seam**: a living interface that incorporates insect tissue into a common host anatomy. Modern breeders cultivate standardized collars around those seams. Early racers pushed raw parts into them; modern competitors buy numbered kits.

**Universal assimilation does not mean unlimited installation.** Any insect lineage can supply a sect, but a finished build still has finite mounting space, fuel throughput, mass tolerance, and legal dimensions. Compatibility is physical and functional—not an arbitrary species-rejection roll.

The named roach survives rebuilds. Standard play uses repairable damage and recovery molts rather than permanent loss of the player’s carefully assembled collection. Breeding expands the catalogue of reproducible bodyforms and donor lines; it does not replace assembly with a hidden-stat lottery.

## 3. Core sect catalogue

The core inventory follows the nine reference slots above. The first six are required; the final three are optional upgrades.

“None” on an optional slot means native roach tissue remains—not a missing body part.

### 3.1 Vehicle/body → **Body kits**

**What the player handles:** the racer’s overall outer form. Separate thoracic frames fit inside it. Every kit includes a standard light carapace; protective replacements are discussed later.

| Body kit       | Appearance and contribution                                                                                   | Its compromise                                             |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Lowline**    | Long, flattened roach body with a shallow dorsal ridge. A low-profile foundation for technical racing.        | Limited belly clearance; scrapes crests and debris.        |
| **Needleback** | Narrow, tapered abdominal segments and a tight shoulder profile. Suits passing gaps and straight-line builds. | Less lateral stability and less room for bulky modules.    |
| **Broadback**  | Wide, squat body with flared sides. Accommodates strong bracing and stable contact builds.                    | More width and mass; poor access to narrow passing routes. |

These are bodyform choices, not increasingly powerful species. A starter Lowline can remain competitive after its internals are rebuilt.

### 3.2 Frame → **Thoracic cradles**

Mini 4WD chassis differ in geometry, weight distribution, access, and attachment layout; they are more than generic durability upgrades. That structural role becomes the roach’s internal load-bearing cradle. ([Tamiya][2])

| Sect module         | Appearance and contribution                                                                                | Best use / drawback                                  |
| ------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| **Roach Flexrail**  | Overlapping amber ribs connected by flexible seams. Permits controlled body flex.                          | Uneven technical tracks; twists under powerful rams. |
| **Scarab Boxbrace** | Thick, interlocking thoracic plates around the drive organs. Resists deformation and supports heavy loads. | Combat and steep climbs; significant mass penalty.   |
| **Locust Truss**    | Sparse, elongated struts with large open spaces between them. Lightweight structural support.              | Speed and jumping; vulnerable exposed joints.        |

**Frame stiffness, landing absorption, and armor are different properties.** A rigid frame can transmit a harsh landing directly into its organs.

### 3.3 Motor → **Drive organs**

The miniature-car equivalent is the compact electric motor. Its defining choices include rotational speed and torque; Tamiya explicitly distinguishes high-RPM, high-torque, and balanced setups. ([Tamiya][3])

| Sect module             | Appearance and contribution                                                                          | Best use / drawback                                                                         |
| ----------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Roach Flat-Six**      | Six muscular lobes arranged around a shared drive ring. Balanced cycle rate, force, and consumption. | Mixed circuits; neither extreme straight-line pace nor exceptional load hauling.            |
| **Cicada Redline**      | A densely ribbed, visibly vibrating thoracic cassette. Very high contraction cadence.                | Long straights; hungry, demanding, and ineffective when insufficiently geared for the load. |
| **Scarab Torque Block** | A squat bundle of thick muscle columns. High working force at lower cadence.                         | Climbing, heavy armor, and contact recovery; lower unloaded speed ceiling.                  |

A motor does **not** define stride length or grip. A Redline attached to unsuitable legs can waste most of its output.

### 3.4 Gears → **Coxal transmissions**

Car gears are intermeshing toothed wheels that change power delivery. RRR’s corresponding parts sit around the hips and alter leverage, effective stride, and synchronization. They do not create energy.

| Sect module                | Appearance and contribution                                                                          | Best use / drawback                                                                       |
| -------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Mole-Cricket Lowbox**    | Short, thick ivory lever arms around the hip sockets. Favors thrust and restart acceleration.        | Climbs, corners, and hauling; shorter effective stride limits straight-line speed.        |
| **Tiger-Beetle Longthrow** | Long, arched linkage forks. Converts each drive cycle into more ground travel.                       | Long straights; weak launch under heavy load.                                             |
| **Issus Lockstep**         | Two tiny intermeshing toothed crescents within a paired coupling. Prioritizes synchronized delivery. | Accurate takeoff and stable recovery; less aggressive specialization than either extreme. |

There is an unusually apt biological reference here: juvenile *Issus* planthoppers possess interlocking gears that synchronize their hind legs. **They are not natural variable-ratio gearboxes**; RRR’s transmission variants are fictional extensions of that visual and mechanical idea. ([University of Cambridge][4])

**The builder’s distinction:** the drive organ determines available output; the transmission determines how that output reaches the limbs.

### 3.5 Battery → **Metabolic reservoirs**

The counterpart to removable cylindrical batteries is a removable abdominal reserve cassette.

**Capacity and maximum delivery rate are separate.** A large reservoir can still starve a demanding motor if its outlet is too restrictive.

| Sect module                   | Appearance and contribution                                                                      | Best use / drawback                                                     |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Roach Reserve**             | Compact, segmented storage lobes tucked beneath the abdomen. Moderate capacity and delivery.     | General-purpose builds; average in both extremes.                       |
| **Honeybee Nectar Crop**      | A taut translucent sac filled with amber fluid. High-rate delivery from a small reserve.         | Sprints and repeated bursts; runs dry early.                            |
| **Waxmoth Fatbody Reservoir** | Pale, layered storage lobes forming a heavy belly cassette. High capacity with measured release. | Endurance; added mass and a delivery ceiling that must match the motor. |

Race-start filling and reserve placement are tunable. A lighter partial fill can help a short heat, but that is a deliberate fuel calculation—not a free advantage.

### 3.6 Tires → **Tarsal contact sets**

Tires are the car’s contact surfaces; roach foot tips fill that role.

Avoid “maximum grip is always best”: Tamiya’s low-friction tires deliberately trade grip for different cornering and jumping behavior. RRR should preserve a grip-versus-release problem without pretending insect legs turn exactly like fixed car axles. ([Tamiya][5])

| Sect module              | Appearance and contribution                                                                  | Best use / drawback                                                  |
| ------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Sticktoe Pads**        | Broad, soft pads beneath the feet. Strong planted-foot traction.                             | Smooth technical surfaces; slower release and dust fouling.          |
| **Tiger-Beetle Needles** | Narrow, hard contact points with minimal pad area. Quick release and precise foot placement. | Clean sprint surfaces; poor purchase on loose material.              |
| **Mole-Cricket Cleats**  | Broad digging toes with short, blunt teeth. Mechanical purchase in crumbly ground.           | Ash and rubble inserts; vibration and wasted effort on smooth metal. |

Stick insects’ distinct friction and adhesive foot pads provide a real anatomical reference for separating planted traction from attachment behavior. The race-specific compounds above remain fictional. ([PLOS][6])

Foot sets may be fitted by front, middle, and rear pair. Mixed contact sets count individually toward cost and mass; visual asymmetry must match the actual build.

### 3.7 Side stabilizer → **Balance assemblies**

Physical Mini 4WD stabilizers can contact the course wall as a car tips. RRR offers both passive bracing and an active sensing alternative, rather than treating stability as invisible extra weight. ([Tamiya][7])

| Sect module              | Appearance and contribution                                                                                  | Best use / drawback                                                                          |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Mantis Counterbraces** | Folded lateral supports that extend when the body begins to roll. Widen the recovery footprint.              | Rough corners and contact; occupy side space and create drag when deployed.                  |
| **Beetle Guardknuckles** | Rounded shell knobs on short, rigid side stalks. Catch a developing tip against the rail.                    | Narrow guided channels; contribute little in a wide, open brawl section.                     |
| **Fly Gyropods**         | Paired club-ended stalks vibrating beside the thorax. Detect rotation and trigger corrective limb movements. | Fast transitions, hops, and flight; consume power and cannot physically stop a large impact. |

Real fly halteres provide rotational information used in flight stabilization; the ground-racing integration is an RRR invention. They are sensors, **not magically stabilizing ballast**. ([UW Homepage][8])

### 3.8 Suspension → **Landing couplers**

This preserves Pocket Circuit’s suspension slot. The separate Mini 4WD mass-damper analogue appears in the advanced catalogue; the two are not silently treated as identical equipment.

| Sect module            | Appearance and contribution                                                                    | Best use / drawback                                              |
| ---------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Pleural Bellows**    | Short, accordion-like membranes around the limb roots. Moderate controlled compression.        | General technical use; limited travel for large drops.           |
| **Locust Springknees** | Thick bowed joints with adjustable elastic bands. Longer landing travel.                       | Jump-heavy courses; insufficient damping causes repeat bouncing. |
| **Scarab Hardstops**   | Compact joints with dense compression pads. Short travel and rapid settling under heavy loads. | Armored builds and small bumps; harsh on major drops.            |

**A spring knee is not a jump ability.** Suspension handles incoming impact; a Hopper module stores and releases an intentional launch.

### 3.9 Front wing → **Browfoils**

The visual equivalent is a small surface mounted ahead of or beside the head. Its purpose is high-speed attitude control—not powered flight.

| Sect module           | Appearance and contribution                                                                              | Best use / drawback                                                       |
| --------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Beetle Browfoil**   | A curved shell blade carried low across the brow. Strong nose hold-down in the game’s aerodynamic model. | Fast ramps and straights; drag and front mounting-space cost.             |
| **Locust Split-Vane** | Two slim wing-cover blades flanking the head. Lighter pitch correction.                                  | Mixed jump circuits; weaker control than the full browfoil.               |
| **Moth Fanbrake**     | A pleated fan that opens at configured braking points. Strong temporary aerodynamic braking.             | Fast approaches to tight sections; deployment delay and large speed loss. |

A stationary foil contributes little at low speed. A Fanbrake slows the racer; it does not become a free speed bonus because its source insect has wings.

These are deliberately stylized aerodynamic effects. Their useful magnitude at the chosen creature scale must be established in the prototype.

## 4. The deeper kit layer: physical Mini 4WD equivalents

These are **hobby-derived extensions**, not additional slots claimed to appear in the Gaiden screenshot. Tamiya’s parts range and tuning sets separately support wheels, shafts/bearings, rollers, brakes, and dampers. ([Tamiya][9])

| Physical car part                          | RRR module family and examples                                                                                                                             | Tuning decision                                                                                                                  |
| ------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Wheel/hub geometry**                     | **Limb spars:** short **Roach Stubs**; long **Tiger-Beetle Stilts**; wide **Scarab Splaylegs**.                                                            | Limb reach and stance versus clearance, turning footprint, and leverage. Foot compounds remain separately fitted.                |
| **Axles, bearings, and drivetrain joints** | **Joint sleeves:** light **Lacewing Sleeves**; flexible **Roach Bushings**; heavy sealed **Beetle Cups**.                                                  | Drive losses versus impact tolerance and resistance to grit.                                                                     |
| **Wall rollers and their mounts**          | **Rail shoes:** slick **Beetle Cheeks**; narrow **Roach Runners**; broad articulated **Scarab Shoes**.                                                     | Following a wall without catching or wasting excessive speed. These slide; they are functional analogues, not biological wheels. |
| **Brake pads**                             | **Ventral brakes:** mild **Moth-Felt Pads**; strong **Stick-Insect Gripstrips**; rough-surface **Cricket Spurs**.                                          | Braking strength, clearance, and which surface engages the brake. Stronger is not universally faster.                            |
| **Mass dampers**                           | **Rebound pods:** compact **Beetle Pearl**; paired **Roach Sidepods**; heavy **Larval Keel**. Each contains a constrained moving mass in a damping sleeve. | Reduced post-landing rebound versus carried mass and occupied space.                                                             |

Tamiya’s sponge brakes address excessive jump speed, while its mass dampers address landing behavior. Preserve that causal separation:

> **Brake before the jump. Absorb the landing. Suppress the rebound.** ([Tamiya][10])

Advanced parts ship as small upgrade packs. Native geometry remains available at zero *additional* Graft Points, already accounted for in the body kit. Every replacement declares its revised cost and mass; nothing is added “for free” through an uncounted accessory menu.

## 5. The derby additions: where RRR becomes its own sport

**Weapons, active jumping, and sustained flight do not have honest 1:1 Pocket Circuit counterparts.** They extend the completed racing foundation.

A beetle horn is not a motor. A mantis claw is not a tire.

### 5.1 Armor: protective carapace replacements

Armor extends the body-shell choice. It does not replace the structural frame.

| Carapace                     | Appearance and purpose                                                           | Trade-off                                                                  |
| ---------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Roach Raceskin — Light**   | Thin, overlapping plates with exposed flexible seams. Basic abrasion protection. | Vulnerable to crushing and concentrated strikes.                           |
| **Scarab Laminate — Medium** | Layered plates along the back and shoulders. Deflects glancing hits.             | More mass; joints and belly remain attackable.                             |
| **Ironclad Mantle — Heavy**  | Interlocking, thick dorsal and lateral slabs. Strong compression protection.     | Slow acceleration, restricted flex, and obstruction of dorsal wing mounts. |

Protection is location-specific. Adding a dorsal plate does not inexplicably protect an exposed foot.

### 5.2 Offensive sects

| Sect / occupied mount                    | Appearance and action                                                                                            | Racing purpose / counterplay                                                                                                                      |
| ---------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Bullhorn Ram — brow**                  | An upward-curving beetle horn rooted in a reinforced forehead saddle. Lifts or displaces a racer directly ahead. | Clears a contested line; needs an aligned approach and enough closing speed. A miss destabilizes the attacker. Competes with browfoils.           |
| **Mantis Catchclaws — paired forelimbs** | Folded, toothed grasping arms snap around a nearby horn or appendage.                                            | Counters a committed ram and spoils a pass. Gripping raises the attacker’s front feet, sacrificing traction and forward progress.                 |
| **Stag Gatecrasher — brow**              | Wide, branching jaws briefly push a neighboring racer off its preferred line.                                    | Useful in broad merges. Its width makes narrow channels hazardous, and holding an opponent also slows the holder.                                 |
| **Bombardier Boiler — rear abdomen**     | Twin reservoir lobes terminate in a short directional outlet. Delivers a limited-range defensive spray.          | Discourages tailgating and attacks exposed modules. Limited ammunition, recovery time, and reservoir-space competition prevent continuous firing. |

The bombardier’s aimed spray has a biological reference; its fictional racing effects and ammunition model are separate design choices. ([PubMed Central (PMC)][11])

Attacks resolve through **position, reach, alignment, wind-up, contact, and recovery**. They are not remote damage rolls. Native biting and body contact remain possible without an offensive sect.

### 5.3 Mobility sects

| Sect / occupied mount                      | Race function                                                                                               | Necessary cost                                                                                             |
| ------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Grasshopper Hoppers — hind limbs**       | Powerful folded rear legs launch the roach over a nearby attacker or onto a marked jump route.              | Fuel expenditure, preload time, interrupted ground drive, and a real landing. Replaces rear running spars. |
| **Click-Beetle Kickback — thoracic hinge** | A loaded hinge snaps the body upright after a rollover. A recovery mechanism, not a controllable long jump. | Stopped or heavily slowed activation; recovery time and structural space.                                  |
| **Dragonfly Quadwing — dorsal assembly**   | Four narrow articulated wings support rapid heading changes and controlled gate approaches.                 | Exposed wings, high active demand, and a matched flight-drive organ. Flyer division only.                  |
| **Locust Longwing — dorsal assembly**      | Long paired flight surfaces favor sustained forward travel.                                                 | Wide turning arcs, takeoff commitment, and a matched flight-drive organ. Flyer division only.              |

Flyer kits retain the same core slots. A **flight-rated drive organ** occupies the Motor slot. A **Wingstroke Yoke**, a paired wing-root lever assembly, occupies the Gears slot and trades wingbeat rate against stroke leverage.

The yoke includes a basic walking output for launch and landing—not a second freely upgradeable ground transmission. Wings are an additional dorsal assembly, never a replacement name for the front aero slot.

Click beetles’ spring-and-latch mechanism supplies the reference for Kickback; reliable racing recovery is the fictional adaptation. ([Illinois News Bureau][12])

The original three friends therefore establish a **conditional counter-triangle**, not hard-coded rock-paper-scissors. A horn can dominate a straight approach; claws can catch that commitment; a hopper can clear both.

But low ceilings defeat the hop, separated lanes remove the grapple opportunity, and a bad landing gives the horn another chance.

### 5.4 Mounting rules that make Frankenstein building interesting

The brow accepts a ram, jaw assembly, **or** browfoil—not all three.

Forelimb weapons may obstruct forward braces and temporarily reduce foot contact. Hoppers replace rear running geometry rather than stacking on top of it. Rear weapon reservoirs compete with metabolic storage. Powered wings require an unobstructed dorsal envelope; a full Ironclad Mantle blocks that envelope.

An incompatible overlap is shown in the assembly preview. An illegal width is shown against the inspection gauge. The player should never discover an invisible compatibility rule after paying for a part.

## 6. Assembly must be a playable activity

**The starter product is a kit—not a completed creature with an equipment screen.**

A kit arrives in a tin with an illustrated lid, a numbered living core, moist sect sprues, labeled part wells, a stain sheet, and an exploded assembly guide. Rare kits can have desirable silhouettes and presentation without secretly superior hidden stats.

The first assembly is hands-on: seat the thoracic cradle, install the drive organ, connect its hip transmission, fit the three limb pairs and contact sets, position the reserve, close the body shell, then install optional handling equipment. Each step is reversible at the bench.

Players adjust **part placement as well as part selection**: stance width, belly clearance, reserve position, brace height, brake clearance, foil angle, and spring preload. The interface shows the consequences immediately, including center of mass and mount conflicts.

A three-stage bench test follows: a slatted treadmill for cadence and slipping; a banked channel for tracking and tipping; a small drop rig for landing and rebound. The same symptoms must later appear on the racecourse.

Finishing matters. Stains, numbers, donor-family markings, shell gloss, and faction insignia turn a collection of parts into a named racer.

A completed build receives a reproducible **build sheet** containing every module, grade, placement, setting, and paint choice. Experienced players may reapply saved assemblies quickly; kit-building depth must not become repetitive compulsory clicking.

## 7. Grades, ratings, and progression

### Three independent classifications

**Part grade** describes the engineered specification. **Race class** describes what kinds of builds may compete. **Circuit tier** describes competitive progression.

None substitutes for another.

| RRR part grade   | Meaning                                                | What improves                                                  |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **I — Stock**    | Standard kit-grown part.                               | Accessible, predictable baseline.                              |
| **II — Select**  | Specimen selected to a published specification.        | A focused improvement, still inexpensive to field.             |
| **III — Tuned**  | Purpose-grown racing variant.                          | Stronger specialization and useful adjustment range.           |
| **IV — Spliced** | Engineered composite retaining a clearly defined role. | More demanding combinations and sharper trade-offs.            |
| **V — Apex**     | Extreme competition specification.                     | Highest specialization, often high cost, demand, or fragility. |

The reference’s Plus and Ultra naming becomes Select and Apex in the worked translation; the complete five-grade ladder is RRR’s own standardized progression.

Grade does not mean “better at everything.” An Apex Redline can be inferior to a Stock Flat-Six on a short, twisting track. Cosmetic rarity and acquisition scarcity remain separate from grade.

### Read the racer—not one total-power number

The main display reports **top speed, acceleration, grip, cornering, balance, hold-down, and mass**. This follows the types of readings visible in the reference customization screen rather than reducing the build to an overall level. ([『龍が如く』公式ポータルサイト｜SEGA][1])

RRR additionally reports **fuel capacity and delivery, predicted consumption, landing travel and damping, protection by body region, and attack or mobility costs**.

Grip is contact purchase. Cornering is how quickly the whole build can negotiate a bend. Balance is resistance to tipping. They are related, not interchangeable.

Each part card needs its donor, slot, grade, Graft Points, mass, physical envelope, power demand, contribution, liability, legal classes, and adjustment range. Ability cards also state wind-up, range, resource cost, and recovery.

**There is no universal synergy bonus for matching insect species.** Useful synergies arise from mechanics: a forceful motor plus a low transmission can haul armor; synchronized hind legs can make a jump more accurate; good feet cannot rescue a landing that exceeds the joints’ travel.

### Three derby conversions

**Horn Tax:** start with a Broadback, Scarab Boxbrace, Scarab Torque Block, Lowbox, Roach Reserve, Cleats, Guardknuckles, Hardstops, and no browfoil. Fit the Bullhorn Ram and medium Scarab Laminate. It should excel at contested climbs and merges, but pay heavily on uninterrupted straights.

**Third Friend:** start with a Lowline, Locust Truss, Flat-Six, Lockstep, Nectar Crop, Sticktoe Pads, Gyropods, Springknees, and Split-Vane. Retain light Raceskin; replace rear spars with Hoppers and add a modest rebound pod. It should win through evasive routing—not out-damaging the field. Its weaknesses are ceilings, repeated jump costs, and congested landings.

**Firework Etiquette:** start with a Needleback, Locust Truss, Redline, Lowbox, Nectar Crop, Needles, Gyropods, short-travel Bellows, and no front aero. Fit a Bullhorn Ram and Bombardier Boiler under light Raceskin. It is an offensive-class sprint build whose weapons compete with its own fuel supply—not an endurance competitor.

### A complete Flyer build

**Skyhook:** Needleback body; Sticktoe Pads for launch and landing; Locust Truss; flight-rated Cicada Redline; Wingstroke Yoke; Roach Reserve matched to the drive’s delivery requirement; Fly Gyropods; soft Pleural Bellows; no front aero.

Add Dragonfly Quadwing and retain light Raceskin. Tune for controlled approaches to closely spaced aerial gates. Its exposed wings and modest reserve make long, open endurance routes a poor matchup.

# PART II — BUILD THE CIRCUIT

## 9. The track is also a kit

Keep the modular-course identity: interlocking lanes, rails, elevation supports, and repeatable section geometry. Tamiya’s actual system includes slot-together straights and separate curve, lane-change, and wave sections. ([Tamiya][13])

RRR supplies battered rain gutters, stamped appliance panels, culverts, fan housings, and industrial ducts—but beneath the dressing, official sections use standardized connectors.

A beautiful scrap-built track must still have readable geometry.

### 9.1 Direct track-module translation

| Pocket/Mini 4WD section or function | RRR module                                                | What it tests                                                  |
| ----------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Straight                            | **Gutter Straight** — smooth metal channel                | Output, gearing, fuel, passing pace.                           |
| Broad curve                         | **Drum Bend** — curved scrap-metal rail                   | Stable speed through sustained turning.                        |
| Tight curve                         | **Valve Hairpin** — compact return bend                   | Braking, clearance, release, and reacceleration.               |
| Wave / alternating bends            | **Radiator Weave** — repeated offsets                     | Rapid balance recovery; an overly wide build catches walls.    |
| Bank                                | **Dish Bank** — tilted channel sections                   | Center of mass, tracking, and speed control.                   |
| Climb                               | **Boiler Rise** — supported uphill panels                 | Thrust under load and usable traction.                         |
| Descent / crest                     | **Spillway Drop** — crest followed by downhill track      | Pitch control and brake setup.                                 |
| Jump section                        | **Broken-Gutter Gap** — specified launch and landing pair | Takeoff speed, trajectory, suspension, and rebound.            |
| Lane changer                        | **Crossover Culvert** — separated over/under exchange     | Transition stability and rotation through lane positions.      |
| Bridge / overpass                   | **Pipe Rack** — elevated crossing with supports           | Clearance, elevation transitions, and compact layout building. |
| Start / lap timing                  | **Release Rack and Tally Gate**                           | Simultaneous release and valid lap recognition.                |

### 9.2 Surface inserts: same shape, different setup

A straight can carry smooth enamel, packed ash, rough perforated plate, or shallow contaminated sludge. The section’s geometry stays recognizable while its grip, fouling, and resistance change.

Every sanctioned track publishes its surface schedule before entry. Wear, moisture, and loose material need visible cues—not random trap tiles disguised as identical flooring.

### 9.3 Derby-only extensions

**Brawl Straight:** internal dividers end inside an unchanged outer track boundary, creating a shared contact zone.

**Merge Funnel:** two routes enter one broad racing space. A slower but cleaner approach may beat a fast route arriving in traffic.

**Low-Clearance Tunnel:** makes tall horns, raised hoppers, and bulky mantles costly. Clearance is measured and displayed.

**Hopper Branch:** an optional launch route with a marked landing gate. It offers different distance or traffic exposure, balanced by fuel, landing, and setup costs.

**Flyer Gate Frame:** an elevated sequence of hoops and containment frames. Flyers must traverse the ordered route rather than fly directly to the finish.

These are RRR additions. A lane changer is not silently redefined as an unregulated intersection, and ordinary track rails do not mysteriously permit attacks through solid walls.

### 9.4 Racing-space rules

**Guided sections preserve Pocket Circuit’s identity; shared sections permit derby interaction.** Official contact courses deliberately alternate both.

A hop is legal over an opponent or into a marked branch, but not across arbitrary track boundaries. Mandatory checkpoints validate course completion. Sustained flight belongs in the Flyer division, including within Open events.

Equal-distance lanes or scheduled lane rotations address permanent inside-lane advantage. Where one crossover rotates three lanes each lap, lap totals cover complete three-lap cycles. Deliberately unequal routes must publish their trade-offs.

Qualifying heats rotate starting positions. Competitors receive the same announced track state.

A course-out requires recovery at the last valid checkpoint with an appropriate loss of time; technical pure-circuit events may instead declare immediate retirement. The event card states which rule applies **before** the race.

## 10. Three example circuits

| Circuit                                   | Modular composition                                                                                                                       | Its lesson                                                                            |
| ----------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Tinpan Eight — Local**                  | Two long straight runs, two broad returns, one Weave, and a lane-changing bridge. Nine short laps across three rotating lanes; no combat. | A fast build still needs enough grip, stability, and fuel to finish.                  |
| **Boilerhouse Scramble — Regional**       | Climb, crest, modest gap, tight return, Brawl Straight, and an optional Hopper Branch. Six laps.                                          | Thrust, landing control, traffic, and route choice can outweigh maximum speed.        |
| **Last Light Grand Circuit — World City** | Long straights, repeated lane exchanges, shared merges, ash inserts, a low tunnel, and two different landing profiles. Multi-heat final.  | No single specialty solves everything; the lanista must tune between published heats. |

Track previews identify the hardest braking approach, steepest rise, lowest clearance, longest fuel demand, and highest-risk landing. Players may test legal builds before committing to the event.

# PART III — RACE THE WASTELAND

## 11. Classes are horizontal; championships are vertical

Your progression remains:

**Locals → Regionals → Factionals → faction representation in New World City → the What’s Left of the World Derby.**

A faction may sponsor, train, and lend parts, but qualification is earned on the track. Its identity changes the community, presentation, and favored engineering—not access to an unbeatable exclusive component.

### Competition classes

| Class                               | Entry rules                                                                                                                                 | Intended racing                                                                                   |
| ----------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Stock / Kit Class**               | Approved complete-kit specifications; no offensive or powered mobility sects.                                                               | Assembly fundamentals and accessible local competition.                                           |
| **Speedsters**                      | No dedicated offensive sects. Native biting and body contact remain legal. Ground-racing movement rules apply.                              | Fast racing with opportunistic contact; no disguised weapon modules.                              |
| **All-Out Offensive — “Fireworks”** | At least one functional major offensive assembly; light Raceskin only. Integrated reinforcement counts toward protection limits.            | Frequent attacks without heavy-armor stalemates.                                                  |
| **Flyers**                          | Sustained-flight builds race only against sustained-flight builds on aerial courses.                                                        | Can carry Speedster, Offensive, or Open equipment rules as a second label.                        |
| **Open — the headline class**       | No Graft Point ceiling and no attack or armor restriction. Physical course envelope, self-contained operation, and checkpoint rules remain. | Extreme combinations, still required to complete a race. Ground Open and Flyer Open are separate. |

**“Anyone can enter Open” describes build freedom—not a World City invitation.** A local builder can enter local Open qualifying; reaching the world event still requires circuit victories and factional qualification.

Stock has its own fixed specifications. Other regulated events can begin testing with **20 / 30 / 40 / 50 Graft Point brackets** across the four competitive tiers.

**Open is exempt from those caps at every tier.**

Fireworks’ offense floor should be based on a functioning inspected attack assembly—not a cosmetic horn or a nominal stat on an unusable weapon. Exact offensive ratings and protection ceilings require playtesting.

## 12. What happens during a race

**The lanista configures; the roach executes.** This is not primarily direct steering.

Before the start, set a simple race plan: preferred legal route, passing aggression, attack trigger, reserve threshold, and allowed jump windows. Beginners choose named presets; advanced players edit conditions.

During a race, the prototype allows a small shared pool of **urge cues**: spend one to request a burst, a fitted mobility action, or a recovery response.

A cue consumes the relevant physical resources. It cannot override geometry, cancel an established hit, or invent an absent module. The pool size is a tuning variable.

Combat always supports a racing objective. Successful attacks can earn spectacle recognition or a side prize, but **normal derby victory belongs to the first valid finisher**, not the highest damage score. Retired competitors are classified by completed checkpoints and progress—not kills.

Changing conditions must be announced and observable. The same build under the same test conditions should produce repeatable diagnostic behavior. Uncertainty comes principally from competitors and disclosed race conditions, not unexplained course-out dice rolls.

## 13. The reward and learning loop

**Race → diagnose → earn a new option → assemble → test → return to beat a specific problem.**

A local rival with a horn introduces contact control. Winning a landing challenge awards a spring or rebound-pod specification. A long regional event introduces reserve planning. Faction workshops offer alternative engineering traditions, while world-level play asks the player to combine familiar systems under pressure.

Rewards include selectable sect packs, reproducible donor cultures, body kits, tuning equipment, practice-track sections, cosmetic trophies, and workshop reputation.

Reward meaningful losses with useful diagnosis and some progress. Do not require the player to win a race to obtain its only viable counter.

The hobby culture belongs at the center: numbered kit tins, breeder catalogues, swap meets, club practice nights, scrutineering benches, local champions’ build sheets, and lovingly maintained racers with years of visible repairs.

**The apocalypse supplies the materials and humor. The kit-building community supplies the affection.**

SEGA’s official Pocket Circuit description includes car-body and part acquisition, Time Attack, and Rival modes. Their RRR equivalents are the sect market, clean-lap workshop challenges, and named lanista rivalries. ([『龍が如く』公式ポータルサイト｜SEGA][14])

## 14. The post-race diagnostic handbook

| Observed failure                         | Plausible causes                                                 | Useful changes to test                                                                  |
| ---------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Fast on the straight; loses every corner | Overcommitted gearing, late braking, unstable stance.            | Shorter transmission, earlier braking, revised braces—not automatically a bigger motor. |
| Runs strongly, then fades                | Reserve exhausted, delivery insufficient, excessive ability use. | Check the consumption trace; change capacity, output, or race plan accordingly.         |
| Lands, then bounces out                  | Too much stored spring energy or too little rebound damping.     | Adjust couplers or add a rebound pod; lowering takeoff speed may be better.             |
| Cannot climb with a horn and armor       | Too much load for current thrust and traction.                   | Shorter leverage, torque-focused drive, suitable feet, or less armor.                   |
| Hops past opponents, loses afterward     | Slow preload, overshoot, unstable landing, long recovery.        | Reduce jump power, improve synchronization, or skip that particular hop.                |
| Wins grapples but loses races            | Contact slows both competitors more than it helps positioning.   | Shorter holds, selective triggers, or a lighter non-grappling build.                    |

The replay should identify the failing section and show the relevant measurements. It should help the player reason—not prescribe one universally correct shopping list.

## Foundational design test

The first playable prototype should prove that a **weaponless roach is satisfying to assemble, tune, and race**. Then introduce **the horn, the claws, and the hoppers** on one shared-track section.

If the fastest kit always wins, handling and course design are too weak. If the biggest weapon always wins, racing has been displaced. If the newest grade always wins, tuning has been displaced. If players cannot explain why a change helped, the feedback is too weak.

**The ideal victory is not “my roach had higher stats.” It is “I built this ridiculous little thing to solve this exact race—and it worked.”**
